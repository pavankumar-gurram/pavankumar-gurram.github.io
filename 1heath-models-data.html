Training: What We Do and Do Not Do

  • 1

    No training on patient data without consent. Any future 1Heath model will learn only from consultations recorded with written consent, reviewed by a doctor and de-identified.

  • 2

    No training on other AI models' output. Text produced by any AI model is used for testing only, never as training data, because a model trained on another model's mistakes repeats them confidently.

  • 3

    Any new model is checked by the same rules. A model can make the draft better; it never replaces verification.

  • 4

    Speech providers must not train on our audio. Retention set to zero and training switched off, confirmed in writing, before any real patient audio is sent.

How We Test

What We Measure, in Order of Importance

  1. Dosing phrases kept exactly (strength, frequency, duration)
  2. Critical errors, including negation flips ("not Dolo" heard as "Dolo")
  3. Medicine names correct, and zero invented medicines
  4. Numbers and units correct
  5. Blocking lines per consultation (target: three or fewer)
  6. General word error rate, last, because a transcript can read well and still get the dose wrong

Held-Out Discipline

Once a test set has been used to tune the system, it no longer counts as evidence. Every claim on this site comes from consultations the system had not seen while being tuned.

What We Measured

Model Plus Rules Beats Either Alone

Brand Names Are Where Speech Fails

Translation Changes Numbers

Translating speech to English helps the wording but corrupts doses. "Azithral 500 once a day" became "once a day for 500 days"; "Pan 40, one" became "Pan 41"; "one in the morning" became "1 AM". The fix: audio chunks that contain a medicine name, a number or a dosing word are also transcribed in the original language, and the model reads both. On recorded consultations this second pass ran on about 62% of audio and raised speech cost from about ₹0.50 to about ₹0.81 a minute[5].

What Published Studies Say About AI-Written Notes

1.47%Sentences in AI-written clinical notes that were made up (191 of 12,999)Clinical safety study[6]
44%Of those made-up sentences could have changed diagnosis or treatment if not correctedSame study[6]
3.45%Sentences from the conversation that were left out of the noteSame study[6]

A rate of 1.47% sounds small, but a note has dozens of sentences, so many notes will contain at least one invented fact. That study used English primary-care conversations; Indian code-mixed consultations are harder. The lesson is the same as ours: an AI draft needs a check that does not depend on the AI.

How Close Is This to the Real World?

Not close enough to make accuracy claims. The synthetic voices come from the same vendor whose speech recognition we use, which flatters the results. Real doctors speak faster, patients interrupt, and clinics are noisy.

This is a problem for the whole field in India. A September 2026 study found that most clinical conversation datasets are simulated or Western, that interruptions were about 12% of turns in Indian simulated data against roughly none in Western datasets, and that Indian scribe vendors each evaluate privately with no shared benchmark[7]. Even in the United States, a 2025 randomised trial found that ambient scribes made clinically significant mistakes occasionally and required ongoing vigilance[8].

Next: record real consultations with written consent from doctors who volunteer, measure every metric above on them, and publish the method and results here, including the failures.
