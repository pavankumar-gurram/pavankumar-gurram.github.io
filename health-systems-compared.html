Key Points
  • The fairest overall comparison, the HAQ index of healthcare access and quality, puts Iceland, Norway and the Netherlands first; India ranked 145th of 195, with Kerala and Goa far ahead of Uttar Pradesh and Assam[1].
  • The best emergency care is a system, not a country: trauma networks cut deaths by 19% to 38% in the US, England and Australia[2][3][4]. In five large Indian hospitals, 58% of trauma deaths were judged preventable[5].
  • The countries with the "most cancer" are mostly the ones that find it; Indians diagnosed with cancer are more than twice as likely to die of it as Americans[6].
  • Thailand and Sri Lanka show what a middle-income country can do with universal coverage, midwives and data[7][8].

How Do You Compare Health Systems?

Spending and rankings of "satisfaction" can mislead. The most useful single comparison counts deaths that should not happen when people get timely, effective care: the Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) index, built by the Global Burden of Disease study for 195 countries[1].

What stands out: India spends 3.3% of its income on health, close to Sri Lanka (3.7%) and Thailand (4.5%). Yet Thailand's families pay only about 10% from their own pockets, against about 44% in India, because public schemes cover almost everyone and pay for most care (how Thailand did it). Spending more is not enough on its own: the United States spends 16.7% and still loses more young children than Sri Lanka.

Which Emergency System Is Best, and Why?

No country is "best" at emergencies. The places that save the most injured people share one design: a trauma system that sends the seriously injured straight to a designated major trauma centre, and a registry that measures every case.

The preventable deaths came mostly from failures to control bleeding and resuscitate (about 16%), airway problems (about 14%), delays before hospital (about 10%), missing protocols and slow imaging[5]. India has laid foundations: bystanders who help are protected from civil and criminal liability and cannot be forced to give their identity[14], and accident victims get up to ₹1.5 lakh of cashless treatment for seven days[15]. What it lacks is the network that sends the patient to the right hospital, a public that knows CPR, and a registry that counts every death.

What the UK Adds: A Shared Summary Record

England's A&E departments miss their own four-hour target, seeing 74.9% of patients in time against a 95% standard[16]. Its lesson for India is different: every GP-registered patient has a Summary Care Record of medicines, allergies and reactions that emergency teams, ambulance crews and pharmacists can see, and that a clinician may open in the patient's best interests when the patient cannot consent[17].

Screening That Worked: South Korea

South Korea offers stomach cancer screening every two years from age 40. Participation rose from 7.4% in 2002 to 62.9% in 2019; the share of cancers found at an early stage rose from 28.6% to 63.6%; five-year survival rose from 43.9% to 77.5%; and people screened by endoscopy had a 47% lower risk of dying from stomach cancer[23]. The lesson for India is not stomach cancer specifically but the method: a national programme, a defined age and interval, and records that recall people.

Roads: Designing Out Death

Sweden adopted Vision Zero in 1997: roads are designed so that a human mistake is not fatal, with roundabouts instead of junctions, barriers between opposing lanes, and lower urban speed limits. Its annual road deaths fell from 541 to about 200[24]. India recorded 1,72,890 road deaths in 2023, with over-speeding linked to 68.1% of them[25].

Thailand: Universal Coverage on a Middle Income

Thailand launched its Universal Coverage Scheme in 2001 and covered its whole population by 2002, enrolling the 4.7 crore people outside the civil service and social security schemes[26].

Households still hit hardest were those using services outside the benefit package or bypassing their registered provider without referral[7]. New households pushed below the poverty line by medical costs fell from 2.71% in 2000 to 0.49% in 2009[26]. Coverage works when there is a clear first point of care and a referral path, both of which depend on records that travel with the patient.

Sri Lanka: Safe Childbirth Without Great Wealth

Sri Lanka's maternal deaths relative to births have halved at least every 12 years since 1935, and skilled attendants assist about 97% of births, up from 30% in 1940. It did this with free health services, rural clinics, professionally trained midwives and systematic use of health data[8]. India's maternal mortality is about 87 per lakh live births[27].

What India Can Take from All of This

  • 1

    Build systems, not just hospitals. Trauma networks, screening programmes and universal coverage all depend on routing the right patient to the right place.

  • 2

    Train the public. Denmark's cardiac-arrest survival roughly tripled as bystander CPR doubled.

  • 3

    Find disease early. Korea's screening turned a mostly late-diagnosed cancer into a mostly early one.

  • 4

    Measure every outcome. Victoria, England and Sri Lanka improved because they counted.

  • 5

    Let the record travel. Every one of these depends on knowing the patient's history at the moment of care.
