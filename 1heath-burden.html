How Much Harm Missing Medical History Causes in India

Key Points
  • India does not count harm caused by missing medical history, so this page estimates it the way epidemiologists estimate dengue or malaria: start with everyone, then narrow down step by step (how many are treated, how many are harmed, how many of those harms come from missing history, how many die), using a published rate at each step[1][2][3].
  • Central estimate: about 6,400 deaths a year in Indian hospitals from preventable medication errors caused by an incomplete history (very likely between 2,900 and 12,800), about 17 a day.
  • In clinics and outpatient care: about 38 lakh medication incidents a year linked to missing history, of which about 1.6 lakh cause severe harm (range 60,000 to 3.4 lakh).
  • By age: about 1,400 of the deaths are children and about 2,000 are people over 65, who are 7% of the population but nearly a third of the deaths, because they take more medicines and are hospitalised far more often for drug harm[4].
  • Suffering that lasts: about 4,600 people a year are left permanently disabled by these errors in hospital alone; most others recover within six months[5].
  • These are estimates with wide ranges, not counts. The method, every input and the code are below so anyone can check or change them.

Why an Estimate, and How Scientists Make One

No Indian register records "harm because the doctor did not know the history". Most errors are never reported, and a death is recorded under the disease, not the error. Epidemiologists face the same gap with dengue and malaria, where most cases are never notified. They do not wait for a perfect count. They build the number from parts that have been measured:

Every input is uncertain, so each is given as a range (low, most likely, high) rather than one number. The model draws 100,000 random combinations from those ranges and reports the middle answer, plus the range that holds 9 in 10 answers. That range is the honest answer: the true number is very likely inside it. Disease-burden studies report uncertainty the same way.

Channel 1: Patients Admitted to Hospital

We start with everyone in India and narrow down, one step at a time, to the people who die because a doctor did not have their medicine history. Each step uses a rate measured in a published study.

Hospital deaths, step by step

  1. People in India146.39 crore
  2. About 3 in 100 of them are treated in hospital in a year2.9%, national health survey4.25 crore
  3. Of those, about 1 in 40 is harmed by a medicine error that could have been prevented2.5%, World Health Organization10.6 lakh
  4. About 1 in 7 of those errors happens because the doctor did not have the patient's history15%, our middle assumption; studies find up to 27%1.59 lakh
  5. About 3 in 100 of those patients die3%; World Health Organization and a Mumbai hospital study4,776 deaths

This uses the most likely value at each step. Because every value is uncertain, we also repeated the calculation 1 lakh times with values drawn from each range. The middle answer was about 6,400 deaths, and 9 in 10 answers fell between 2,900 and 12,800.

Channel 2: Clinics and Outpatient Care

Most Indians are treated in clinics, not hospitals. Here the studies measure safety incidents per consultation and the share causing severe harm, but not deaths, so this channel reports severe harm only.

Most Indians are treated in clinics, not hospitals. The same narrowing works here, but studies of clinics measure severe harm rather than deaths.

Children, Adults and Older People

Harm is not spread evenly by age. To split it, each age group's share of the population is weighted by how much more likely its members are to be harmed by medicines compared with adults aged 15 to 64.

Older people take more medicines and are far more likely to be hospitalised because of one. So we weight each age group by its size and by how much more likely it is to be harmed.

The age split is the least certain part of the model. The risk figures for older people come from the United States, and India has no national data on drug harm by age. The ranges are wide for that reason.

Lasting Harm: People Still Suffering After Treatment

Many people harmed by a medication error are treated and recover. Some are not. The best long-term evidence on what happens to hospital patients after an adverse event is the Harvard Medical Practice Study, which reviewed 30,121 records from 51 New York hospitals in 1984: 70.5% of adverse events caused disability lasting under six months, 2.6% caused permanent disability and 13.6% led to death[5].

What happens to the people harmed in hospital

  1. Hospital patients harmed each year by these errorsmiddle answer of the model1,76,000
  2. About 7 in 10 recover within six months70.5%, Harvard Medical Practice Study1.24 lakh
  3. About 1 in 40 are left permanently disabled2.6%, same study; range 2,300 to 7,900about 4,600

The Harvard study found that 13.6% of adverse events ended in death. Our model assumes far fewer (1.9% to 6.4%), so our death figure is on the cautious side.

In clinics, the 1.6 lakh severe harms a year are by definition the ones that cause serious or lasting damage[3]. Combined with the hospital figures, an incomplete medical history leaves about 1.6 lakh Indians a year with severe or permanent harm from a medicine error, on top of the deaths.

Is the Number Plausible?

A good estimate should sit sensibly inside larger, independently measured numbers.

  • Against the WHO figure. WHO counts 26 lakh deaths a year from unsafe hospital care in low- and middle-income countries[9]. Our hospital estimate covers only one cause (missing history) and one type of harm (medicines).
  • Against Indian hospital data. In one Mumbai emergency department, 0.83% of all admitted patients died from a drug reaction[8]; our model assumes far fewer deaths per admission because most Indian admissions are less severe than a tertiary emergency.

What This Estimate Does Not Do

  • It is not a count. It is a structured estimate. The range is the honest answer; the middle figure is a summary.
  • It leaves out harm we cannot quantify yet: wrong diagnoses because earlier test results were unavailable, emergencies where no one knows the blood group or allergies, Ayurvedic and homeopathic products the doctor never hears about, and families who never learn of an inherited disease.
  • It counts no deaths in outpatient care, because no reliable death rate per outpatient incident exists. The true death toll is therefore higher than the hospital figure alone.
  • Several inputs come from other countries. Indian rates are likely higher, because Indian clinics have less time and fewer records; we did not inflate them.
