Design Principles

  • 1

    Zero extra work for the doctor. The doctor talks as usual; the record writes itself.

  • 2

    The doctor decides. Nothing is signed by the software. Anything uncertain is shown for the doctor to confirm.

  • 3

    Nothing invented, nothing lost. The system never fills in a drug, strength or dose that was not said. Anything it is unsure of is flagged, never dropped.

  • 4

    Indian reality first. Code-mixed speech, brand-name prescribing, two-minute visits, ceiling fans and corridor noise, AYUSH alongside modern medicine.

  • 5

    One record, many views. Each person in the visit gets their own app on the same consented record.

  • 6

    No side between systems. 1Heath records what patients take from every system and never tells anyone to stop or start one.

One Visit, Step by Step

How a Line Earns Trust

Every prescription line ends in one of three states. The doctor sees the doctor's own words next to what the system understood.

StateMeaningDoctor's action
VerifiedEvery part matches what was said and the catalogueNothing
ConfirmA name, strength, dose, frequency or duration is uncertain or missingOne tap on a choice; none is pre-selected
Cannot SignConflicts with a recorded allergy, or a banned combinationChange the line

The checks cover allergies, pregnancy, children's doses by weight, duplicates (including medicines the patient already takes), weekly drugs written as daily, banned combinations and look-alike brand names. The full rule list is on the architecture page.

Alternatives We Compared

We did not start with this design. We tested the obvious options on the same unseen simulated consultations[1].

OptionWhy not, on its own
Doctor types the historyAdds typing to a visit that lasts about two minutes
Human scribesA salary many small clinics cannot carry, and no shared record after the visit
A language model aloneCaptures the most, but adds facts nobody said. One invented allergy or dose is unacceptable
Rules aloneNever invents, but misses most of the conversation and misread who refused what
Global scribesBuilt for English, long visits and insurance billing, not code-mixed speech, brand names or AYUSH
Model plus rules (1Heath)The model understands the sentence; the rules decide whether each line can be trusted; the doctor resolves the rest

Why We Think It Is the Best Available Design, and What Could Prove Us Wrong

The case for it. Missing medicines are the most common history error[2], so the record must be written at every visit without effort. Invented clinical facts are the biggest risk of language models, so every line must be checked against the words spoken. Uncertainty must go to the doctor as a choice, because a guess that looks confident is worse than a question. No single component does all three; the combination does.

What is still unproven. Every result so far uses synthetic voices[1]. Real clinics are noisier, patients interrupt, and accents vary. Other Indian scribes are already in daily use[3], and India still has no shared, real-world benchmark for comparing them[4]. Until 1Heath is measured on consented real consultations, "best" is a design argument, not a measured fact.

Beyond the Consultation

Emergency Card

Blood group, allergies, long-term conditions, current medicines from every system and two family contacts, on the phone's lock screen and as a QR code, readable without unlocking. The patient chooses what it shows.

Family Health

With each person's consent, a family's records link. When one member is diagnosed with an inherited condition such as thalassaemia trait, the doctor sees which relatives should be offered testing, with counselling first.

Watch Data

Patients who connect a smartwatch can share sleep, steps, active minutes and resting heart rate as simple graphs, computed the same way for every brand, shown as context and never as a diagnosis.

What 1Heath Is Not

  • Not a diagnosis tool: the doctor diagnoses; 1Heath records and checks.
  • Not a replacement for clinical judgement: every prescription is the doctor's, signed by the doctor.
  • Not a data business: patient data is never sold.
  • Not ready for real patients yet: see what must happen first.
