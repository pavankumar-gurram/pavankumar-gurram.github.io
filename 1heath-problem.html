Why the history is missing, who it hurts, how big the problem is, and why earlier attempts have not fixed it.

What a Usable History Means

At the moment of care, a doctor needs answers to six questions. In most Indian clinics the answer to each is "whatever the patient remembers", or a plastic bag of old papers.

  1. Allergies. What is this patient allergic to, and what reaction did it cause?
  2. Current medicines. What do they take now, from every system: modern medicine, Ayurveda, homeopathy, Unani, Siddha, home remedies and supplements?
  3. Past illness. Which illnesses, operations and hospital stays have they had?
  4. Earlier tests. What did previous blood tests and scans show?
  5. Family history. What runs in the family: diabetes, heart disease, thalassaemia, cancer?
  6. Pregnancy. For a woman, is she pregnant or could she be? This changes what can be prescribed.

Five People, Five Fragments

Every outpatient visit involves up to five people. Each holds part of the story and none holds all of it.

Why It Happens

There Is No Time to Take a History

A systematic review of consultation times in 67 countries found that primary-care visits in India last about two minutes[1][3]. In two minutes a doctor can examine and prescribe. There is no time to ask six questions, let alone type the answers.

Too Few Doctors for Too Many Patients

India has 13,88,185 registered allopathic doctors and 7,51,768 registered AYUSH practitioners. The government's doctor-to-population ratio of 1:811 assumes 80% of them are available and counts both groups together[4]. Busy clinics see dozens of patients in a session, which is what keeps visits short.

Paper Does Not Travel

Prescriptions are handwritten slips that leave with the patient. Test reports are printed and filed at home, if kept at all. Most small clinics keep no structured digital record, so the next doctor starts from nothing.

Care Is Split Across Providers and Systems

A patient may see a local clinic, a specialist, a hospital, a pharmacist who advises directly, and an Ayurvedic or homeopathic practitioner. In 2022–23, 46.3% of rural and 52.9% of urban Indians used an AYUSH system or home remedy in the past year[5]. None of these providers share a record.

Some Medicines Are Never Mentioned

Many patients do not think of a churna, kashayam or homeopathic globule as a medicine, so they do not mention it unless asked, and in a two-minute visit they are rarely asked. Some of these preparations interact with modern drugs or harm the liver. In one Indian case series, 8 patients developed liver injury from single-ingredient ashwagandha products; 5 had underlying chronic liver disease, and 3 died[6].

In an Emergency There Is No History at All

An accident victim often arrives with a stranger and no record: no blood group, no allergies, no list of medicines. India recorded 4,80,583 road accidents and 1,72,890 road deaths in 2023, about 20 deaths every hour[7][8].

How Big the Problem Is

India does not count harm caused by missing history. The best evidence comes from hospitals, which keep records and employ pharmacists, so small clinics are very likely worse.

The most common error is not a wrong medicine but a missing one. That is exactly the error a record written from the conversation, and shared across visits, is designed to prevent.

How Often Medicines Harm Indian Patients

16.2%In-patients in one north Indian hospital who had at least one adverse drug reaction during their stay (1,033 patients)Ethiopian J Health Sci, 2013[9]
36.2%Of those reactions, judged probably or definitely preventableSame study[9]

The same paper cites a Mumbai study in which adverse drug reactions caused 6.9% of all hospital admissions[9]. Single-hospital studies are not national rates, but they agree on the pattern: a large share of medicine harm is preventable, and prevention starts with knowing what the patient already takes and has reacted to.

How much harm does this add up to? We combined these and other published rates into a national estimate, with every formula and range shown: about 6,400 hospital deaths and 1.6 lakh severe harms a year from medication errors caused by an incomplete history. See the burden estimate →

Most Care Happens Outside Any Shared Record

  • Private care. In the 2017–18 national health survey, about 55% of people who sought treatment went to private facilities, 52% in rural areas[10]. Their records stay inside each clinic unless the clinic chooses to connect to a shared system.
  • No cover. More than 80% of Indians had no health expenditure coverage at the time, and about 80% of rural families paid for hospital stays from savings[10].
  • No rule to keep outpatient records. India's medical ethics regulations require doctors to keep in-patient records for three years and to hand them over within 72 hours when asked. They set no such rule for outpatient visits, and only say efforts should be made to computerise records[11]. Most consultations are outpatient visits, so most leave nothing behind but the patient's slip.

Why Earlier Attempts Have Not Fixed It

ApproachWhat it does wellWhat is still missing
Clinic software with typingStructured records for doctors who typeAdds work to a two-minute visit; the pharmacy, patient and family stay outside it

Why Now

  • Speech in Indian languages works. Speech in Indian languages, including mixes such as Telugu or Hindi with English, can now be transcribed well enough to draft a record, and scribes are already in daily use[12].
  • The rails exist. ABHA health IDs, ABDM consent and exchange[15], and a national drug registry launched in 2026 with more than 1.23 lakh branded and 10,000 generic medicines[16].
  • Doctors want less typing. In a 2025 randomised trial of 238 physicians, ambient scribes were linked to lower work exhaustion, though the time saved was modest and occasional inaccuracies needed vigilance[17].

The Founder's Own Case

I was once given a drug that caused a bad reaction. I still do not know what it was or what dose, so no doctor treating me in future can avoid it. For almost every Indian, that is how medical history works today.

What This Page Does Not Claim

  • There is no national count of deaths caused by missing history. The burden page builds an estimate from published rates, with its uncertainty shown; it is an estimate, not a count.
  • The hospital studies show the pattern; they are not Indian national rates.
  • The ashwagandha case series shows that harm happens; it does not show how common it is.
