High-Level Design

One Consultation, End to End

  1. Swipe to start. Consent is already recorded at the desk. Listening begins.
  2. Capture and chunk. The phone records 16 kHz mono audio; a voice-activity detector cuts it at natural pauses into chunks of up to about 10 seconds.
  3. Transcribe. Each chunk goes through the gateway to speech recognition in translate mode, which returns English in natural word order.
  4. Gate. If the English contains a catalogue name, a molecule, a number or a dosing word, the same audio is sent once more in code-mixed mode to keep the original words and numbers. The last chunk after Stop is sent to both at once.
  5. Live strips. As chunks return, the screen fills two strips: "Already taking" (what the patient says they take) and "Today's orders".
  6. Draft. On Stop, both transcripts go to the drafting model, which returns a structured note: complaints, history, examination, diagnosis, current medicines with an action, and prescription lines with every field.
  7. Verify on the phone. The rules engine checks every line and assigns a state: verified, confirm, or cannot sign.
  8. History paragraph. Written only from verified facts.
  9. Fallback. If the model fails or takes longer than 10 seconds, a rules-only note is shown and every medicine needs confirmation.
  10. Review and sign. The doctor resolves flagged lines and slides to sign.
  11. Store and print. The signed record, each line's spoken words, its interpretation, every reason given and the catalogue source are stored; the prescription PDF is generated.

Cost and Connectivity

QuestionAnswer
Speech cost per consultationAbout ₹0.50 a minute for translation alone, about ₹0.81 a minute with the second original-language pass[1]. A two- to five-minute consultation costs roughly ₹1 to ₹4 in speech fees.
Drafting cost per consultationNot yet measured; it will be measured on recorded consultations before any pricing decision.
What if the internet is slow or down?The demo needs internet to transcribe. If the drafting model fails or takes longer than 10 seconds, the doctor gets a rules-only note in which every medicine must be confirmed. There is no offline mode yet; whether one is needed is a question for the pilot.
Can the model run on India's own infrastructure?Yes in principle: the drafting model's weights are open[2], so it can be self-hosted if a hospital or the law requires it.

Low-Level Design: Matching Medicine Names

  • A spoken word becomes a confirmed name only if it matches exactly, or is close in both spelling (score of at least 0.80) and sound (at least 0.90), and beats the next candidate by at least 0.10.
  • Ordinary English words are never replaced: "shall call" can only suggest Shelcal, never become it.
  • Suffixes are part of the name. Pan and Pan-D, or Telma and Telma-H, are different medicines; a suffix is never dropped or added.
  • Merged numbers are split only when the catalogue confirms the strength: "Pan 40100" becomes Pan 40, 1-0-0.
  • Uncertain names become up to three one-tap choices, each shown with its composition. 90 sourced look-alike pairs always appear together. Context may rank choices, never fill one in.
  • The matching list covers about 500 common outpatient brands with spoken forms in English, Hindi and Telugu and spellings in five Indian scripts (4,649 spellings), plus the clinic's own stock list.

How It Is Built and Tested

  • Rulings live in the repository. Every product decision is written down, and a build checklist fails the build if a rule is broken.
  • About 1,788 automated tests across the apps and the engine, plus image tests that guard approved screens at normal size, 200% text, tablet and stress layouts.
  • A frozen clinical test set of 131 cases; any change to an existing test needs the founder's written acceptance.
  • A benchmark harness that replays cached transcripts, so most accuracy changes are measured without new speech costs.
  • Done means verified on a real Android phone through the real app with a real gesture.
